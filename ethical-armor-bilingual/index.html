Theory after Milestone 1 · Deep dive

Material selection creates supply-chain consequences

Engineering does more than choose a material property. By freezing a specification, an interface and a qualification route, it can also narrow the future set of suppliers, processing routes and origins that Procurement is able to use.

ARIA · Core ideaResponsible sourcing is not “buy the expensive option”. It combines technical qualification, evidence-based due diligence and design choices that preserve credible future sourcing options.
1 · Why Engineering matters

Design freeze can create path dependency

A material decision can become difficult to reverse once geometry, loads, coatings, heat treatment, interfaces, manufacturing processes and qualification evidence are built around it. The longer the product remains in service, the more expensive a late material change may become.

That means a sourcing problem discovered years later may actually originate in an early engineering decision. If only one material grade, one proprietary process or one qualified source can satisfy the frozen design, Procurement has limited leverage when a disruption or social-risk issue appears.

Questions before freeze

  • Does the requirement define the performance needed, or does it unnecessarily lock a specific material/process?
  • Is there more than one technically credible supply route?
  • Can origin and processing information be obtained with enough confidence for due diligence?
  • Would a future change require local requalification, or a platform-wide redesign?
2 · Critical raw materials

“Critical” describes supply exposure — not morality

The EU Critical Raw Materials Act frames critical raw materials around economic importance and supply risk, and aims to improve security, resilience, resource efficiency and circularity across the value chain. A material may be “critical” because supply is concentrated, demand is growing quickly or alternatives are difficult — not because the material itself is unethical.

For an engineer, this changes the design question. The issue is no longer only “does this material meet strength, temperature or magnetic performance?” but also “what dependency does this choice create over decades of production and support?”

Engineering translation: criticality is a design-resilience input. It should trigger questions about material intensity, substitution potential, recycled feedstock, repairability, stock strategy and multi-source compatibility — always within safety and qualification constraints.
Technical lens

Can it perform?

Strength, fatigue, corrosion, temperature, magnetic performance, manufacturability, reliability, certification and configuration control remain non-negotiable. Ethical Armor never asks the learner to bypass those requirements.

Supply lens

Can it remain available?

Look at concentration risk, sole-source exposure, long lead times, processing bottlenecks, geopolitical vulnerability and whether a second route could be qualified without major redesign.

Due-diligence lens

Can we understand the chain?

Traceability, supplier transparency, credible origin information, audit coverage and the ability to act on risks determine whether the organisation can perform meaningful due diligence rather than simply collect declarations.

Regulatory boundary: Regulation (EU) 2017/821 establishes due-diligence obligations for covered Union importers of tin, tantalum, tungsten and gold (3TG). It should not be presented as a blanket legal rule for titanium or all rare-earth materials. This game uses the broader OECD minerals due-diligence method for the fictional titanium/rare-earth case.
01
Engineering example.

Two routes meet the same mechanical requirement. Route A is slightly slower but offers strong upstream evidence and a qualified alternative processor. Route B is faster and cheaper but origin evidence stops at the refiner. The right first action is not “choose A because it sounds ethical”; it is to understand the unresolved evidence, assess material criticality and preserve technically qualified options before freezing the architecture.

Knowledge check

If a material is cheaper and technically superior, does that automatically make it the best engineering choice? No. Technical value must be considered together with lifecycle availability, traceability, qualification constraints and material-dependency risk.

Learning basis: OECD minerals due-diligence guidance; Regulation (EU) 2017/821; Regulation (EU) 2024/1252. Formal applicability must always be validated for the real programme and legal context.

Theory after Milestone 2 · Deep dive

Traceability is a chain of evidence

A robust answer is not “our Tier 1 supplier is European”. A component may pass through alloy producers, traders, processors, smelters/refiners and extraction sites before it reaches Tier 1. What matters is whether the evidence remains coherent across those transformations.

ARIA · Core ideaTraceability is not a map with five boxes. It is the ability to connect material identity, custody, transformation and origin using evidence that is consistent enough to support a decision.
1 · Chain of custody

Who had the material, and what happened to it?

Regulation (EU) 2017/821 defines a chain-of-custody or supply-chain traceability system as a record of the sequence of entities that possess minerals and metals as they move through the supply chain. In practice, an aerospace material route can also involve important transformations: ore becomes concentrate, metal, alloy, billet, forging, powder, magnet, semi-finished product and finally a component.

Each transformation creates a potential evidence break. The investigator therefore needs to connect the incoming material, the outgoing material and the responsible entity at each stage.

2 · Evidence quality

Not all documents answer the same question

A supplier questionnaire may describe policy. A purchase order may identify a commercial relationship. A material certificate may identify grade and heat/lot data. A refiner record may support processing history. An independent audit may evaluate controls. None of these, by itself, proves every upstream fact.

The important question is: what claim does this document actually support? Good due diligence avoids using a document outside its evidentiary scope.

Evidence typeWhat it may supportTypical limitation
Supplier self-declarationPolicy, declared origin, declared upstream controlsMay not be independently verified
Material / batch certificateMaterial identity, grade, lot or heat continuityMay stop at the processor and not show extraction origin
Chain-of-custody recordSequence of entities and custody/transformationCan still contain gaps or unmatched identifiers
Independent audit / assuranceWhether specified controls were tested within a stated scopeScope, date and sampled sites matter; “audited” is not universal proof
Extraction-site evidenceMine/site identity and upstream origin evidenceMust still be matched to the actual batch or material route
3 · Common gap

Identity gap

The paperwork exists, but identifiers do not reconcile: different batch numbers, dates, quantities or material descriptions. The chain may be real, but the evidence does not reliably connect this specific material.

4 · Common gap

Origin gap

The refiner or trader is known, but the extraction source cannot be identified with confidence. This is exactly the contradiction used in Milestone 2.

5 · Common gap

Assurance gap

The supplier states that due diligence is complete, but the audit is outdated, narrow in scope, limited to one facility or unable to test the highest-risk upstream stage.

6 · Common gap

Temporal gap

A once-valid assessment may no longer represent the current route after a supplier, ownership, mine, processor, country context or security situation changes.

Five questions for every node: Who is the entity? What did it receive? What did it transform or transfer? What evidence connects incoming and outgoing material? What due-diligence control covered the relevant risk and time period?
02
Why Milestone 2 mattered.

Tier 1 declared “upstream traceability complete”, yet the refiner evidence could not map the batch to a single extraction source. The contradiction is not proof of abuse. It is proof that the confidence level claimed by Tier 1 is not supported by the available evidence — therefore more investigation is required.

Knowledge check

Can a chain be fully mapped and still have weak traceability? Yes. A diagram can be complete while the evidence behind one or more links is incomplete, inconsistent, outdated or too broad to support the claimed origin.

Learning basis: OECD five-step framework, including management systems, traceability/controls, risk assessment, risk response, audit and reporting; Regulation (EU) 2017/821 definition of chain of custody/traceability.

Theory after Milestone 3 · Deep dive

Red flags are triggers for due diligence — not verdicts

Good investigators separate context, indicators, verified findings and assumptions. That prevents two opposite errors: ignoring meaningful warning signs and treating every warning sign as proof of misconduct.

ARIA · Core ideaA red flag changes the level of scrutiny. It tells you where normal evidence is no longer enough and where enhanced due diligence may be required.
Context

Where is the chain operating?

Conflict-affected and high-risk areas can include armed conflict, fragile post-conflict settings, weak governance or security, and widespread or systematic violations of international law. Context elevates risk; it does not by itself prove that a specific supplier committed abuse.

Supplier / entity

Who are we dealing with?

Unknown beneficial ownership, unexplained intermediaries, repeated refusal to provide basic records, audit scope gaps or inconsistent declarations can justify stronger verification.

Transaction

What does the money or material flow show?

Unusual payment routes, unexplained cash-like fees, mismatched quantities, origin changes, undocumented traders or contradictory tax/royalty information may require escalation.

OECD Annex II risk families

What serious risks does responsible-mineral due diligence look for?

The OECD model supply-chain policy identifies several categories that companies should assess and address in conflict-affected and high-risk mineral chains.

Risk familyExamples of what the framework looks forWhy it matters
Serious abusesForced labour, worst forms of child labour, torture, widespread sexual violence, other gross abuses, war crimes or crimes against humanityThese are among the most severe adverse impacts in the framework
Support to non-state armed groupsPayments, equipment, logistics or sourcing relationships that directly or indirectly support armed groups controlling mines, routes or trade pointsMineral trade can contribute to conflict financing
Public / private security forcesIllegal control, taxation or extortion; inadequate accountability or screeningSecurity arrangements can create or intensify human-rights risk
Bribery / false originBribes or fraudulent misrepresentation used to hide origin, taxes, fees or royaltiesFalse information destroys the reliability of the due-diligence system
Money laundering / revenue transparencyMoney-laundering risk or failure to account for taxes, fees and royaltiesFinancial opacity can conceal conflict-linked or illicit flows
Indicator → finding

Build the evidence chain

An indicator should generate a question: “what additional evidence would confirm or refute this?” A finding requires evidence with a defined scope, source and level of confidence. The organisation should document what is known, what remains uncertain and why the uncertainty matters.

This is especially important in engineering reviews: a decision should not be based on reputation, assumptions about a country, or a colour-coded dashboard with no supporting evidence.

Finding → response

Match the action to the severity

Possible responses include requesting stronger evidence, increasing audit scope, creating a measurable mitigation plan, using leverage with the supplier, temporarily suspending trade, or disengaging when serious risk cannot be acceptably mitigated. The OECD framework explicitly recognises different response strategies depending on the risk and the company's position in the chain.

Do not confuse risk geography with guilt. High-risk context is a reason for enhanced due diligence. It is not a licence to stereotype entire countries, communities or suppliers.
03
Example.

A supplier operates in a high-risk region but provides current site-level evidence, transparent ownership, independent assessment and a functioning grievance process. Another supplier operates in a lower-risk region but refuses to identify upstream traders and has conflicting origin records. The second case may create the stronger immediate due-diligence concern.

Knowledge check

What is the purpose of a red flag? To trigger a deeper, proportionate investigation and risk response — not to act as proof of wrongdoing.

Learning basis: OECD Due Diligence Guidance Annex I and Annex II; the EU Conflict Minerals Regulation uses conflict-affected/high-risk concepts for its covered 3TG scope.

Theory after Milestone 4 · Deep dive

Human-rights due diligence is an ongoing management process

The goal is not to award a permanent “ethical supplier” label. It is to understand actual and potential impacts, act on the most serious risks, track whether controls work, communicate appropriately and provide or cooperate in remediation where responsibility requires it.

ARIA · Core ideaPeople can be affected long before a material reaches aerospace production. Good due diligence asks whose rights may be affected, how severe the impact could be, what evidence exists and what leverage the company can use.
UN Guiding Principles logic

From policy commitment to action

The UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights establish the corporate responsibility to respect human rights and frame human-rights due diligence as a process for identifying and addressing actual and potential adverse impacts. In practice, that means building the process into normal business and engineering decisions rather than treating it as a one-off sustainability questionnaire.

1Assess actual and potential human-rights impacts.
2Integrate findings into relevant functions and act.
3Track whether the response is effective.
4Communicate how impacts are being addressed.
5Enable or cooperate in remediation where appropriate.
Severity

Prioritise the most serious impacts

Not every problem has the same human consequence. A credible process considers the scale of harm, how many people may be affected and whether the harm can be remedied. Severe risks should not be downgraded merely because the supplier is strategically important or the component is urgent.

Affected people

Go beyond the direct workforce

Potentially affected groups may include mine workers, contractor labour, nearby communities, artisanal miners, women and children, Indigenous Peoples, land users and people exposed to private or public security arrangements. A supply-chain assessment that only asks about Tier 1 employees can miss the most serious upstream impacts.

Labour rights

Fundamental rights at work

The ILO's fundamental principles and rights at work cover freedom of association and collective bargaining, elimination of forced labour, abolition of child labour, elimination of discrimination, and a safe and healthy working environment. These provide an important lens for evaluating extraction and processing risks.

Communities and land

Land, resources and meaningful consultation

Where Indigenous Peoples may be affected, international human-rights standards place particular importance on rights connected to land, resources, culture and participation. The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples includes free, prior and informed consent provisions in several contexts. For training, the key lesson is that a land dispute cannot be reduced to “the permit exists”; the quality of consultation, rights affected and unresolved grievances matter.

Engineering relevance: if a design can only use a material route associated with unresolved severe risks, Engineering may have more leverage than it appears. Changing material intensity, opening an alternative qualified route or redesigning an interface can reduce dependency and make mitigation more credible.
EvidenceWeak interpretationBetter due-diligence question

04
Example.

If a credible allegation of forced labour appears upstream, the correct response is not simply “ask Procurement for a cheaper supplier”. The organisation needs to assess the evidence, severity and connection to the chain, then determine the appropriate action under its due-diligence process — potentially including urgent suspension or disengagement for severe risks, while preserving safe technical alternatives.

Knowledge check

Does an audit certificate end human-rights due diligence? No. Due diligence is ongoing and must react to changing circumstances, new evidence and the effectiveness of mitigation.

Learning basis: UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (OHCHR); ILO Declaration on Fundamental Principles and Rights at Work; UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples; OECD minerals guidance.

Theory after Milestone 5 · Deep dive

Mineral value flows can create conflict-financing risk

The concern is not that every payment near an extraction site is suspicious. The concern is whether mineral extraction, transport or trade provides direct or indirect support to actors involved in illegal control, extortion, serious abuses or conflict.

ARIA · Core ideaFollow the beneficiary, purpose and legal basis of the payment. The word “security” is not evidence by itself; neither is a payment automatically illicit because security forces are involved.
1 · Follow the value

Who receives the economic benefit?

Trace payments, fees, taxes, royalties, transport charges, security contracts and trader margins. Identify the beneficiary, ownership, contract, legal basis and reason for payment. Where an intermediary is used, determine what service it actually provides and who ultimately controls it.

The OECD framework distinguishes legitimate, legally required taxes, fees and royalties from support to non-state armed groups or security forces involved in illegal control, taxation or extortion. Therefore, the analysis must be evidence-based.

2 · Follow the control

Who controls mines, routes and trade points?

Conflict-financing risk can arise where armed groups or abusive security forces control access to mine sites, transport routes, trading points or upstream companies; illegally tax or extort money or minerals; or receive logistical assistance, equipment or other support from the mineral trade.

A technically compliant material can therefore still carry a serious sourcing risk if the economic chain contributes to these activities.

Signal A

Unexplained intermediary

A payment passes through an entity with unclear ownership, no clear service and no transparent contractual purpose. This requires investigation of the beneficiary and transaction rationale.

Signal B

Security payment

The term itself is not enough. Ask whether the security force is lawfully engaged, screened, accountable, proportionate and linked to any illegal control, extortion or abuse.

Signal C

False origin / payments

Bribery, fraudulent origin records, misrepresented royalties or laundering of mineral revenues can undermine the entire traceability system and conceal higher-risk links.

OECD response logic

Some risks require stronger responses than ordinary supplier improvement

The OECD model policy recommends immediate suspension or discontinuation of engagement where there is a reasonable risk of sourcing from or linkage to parties committing serious abuses, or of direct/indirect support to non-state armed groups. For certain security-force, bribery, money-laundering and payment-transparency risks, the framework provides for measurable mitigation plans and escalation where improvement fails.

Training point: the exact response depends on the risk category, evidence, the company's position in the chain and applicable internal/legal procedures. Ethical Armor teaches the logic of escalation; it is not a substitute for a real legal determination.
05
Milestone logic.

Knowledge check

Is every government tax or security payment a conflict-financing risk? No. The due-diligence question is whether payments are lawful, transparent and proportionate, and whether they support or are connected to illegal control, extortion, serious abuse or prohibited armed-group support.

Learning basis: OECD Annex II model supply-chain policy, including serious abuses, armed-group support, security-force risks, bribery/false origin, money laundering and taxes/fees/royalties.

Theory after Milestone 6 · Deep dive

Eco-design can redesign the sourcing problem

Ethical sourcing becomes stronger when Engineering reduces structural dependency instead of leaving Procurement to manage a design that has only one technically possible supply route.

ARIA · Core ideaThe most resilient supply chain is often the one the architecture does not over-constrain. Engineering can preserve future sourcing options without relaxing safety, performance or qualification.
Lever 1

Reduce material intensity

Revisit geometry, topology, magnetic architecture, thickness, yield loss and manufacturing route to determine whether the same function can be achieved with less of a constrained material. The objective is not “use less at any cost”; it is to remove unnecessary dependence while preserving technical margins.

Lever 2

Design for substitution

Where feasible, specify the performance envelope and interfaces so future alternatives can be evaluated without redesigning the whole system. This can include alternative grades, processing routes, recycled feedstock or material families — but only after the required qualification and safety evidence.

Lever 3

Modularity

Concentrate material-intensive functions in replaceable modules so future substitutions, obsolescence fixes or alternative sourcing routes remain local rather than propagating through the complete platform architecture.

Lever 4

Multi-source compatibility

A technically open interface can make it possible to qualify more than one credible route. This improves resilience and bargaining leverage, but it requires configuration discipline so alternatives remain equivalent and controlled.

Lever 5

Circularity and secondary supply

The Critical Raw Materials Act explicitly links secure supply with resource efficiency and circularity. Depending on the material and aerospace requirements, recycled content, recovery, reuse or closed-loop strategies may reduce primary-material exposure — but aerospace quality, contamination, provenance and certification constraints still apply.

Lever 6

Design for information

Material traceability is easier when the product definition preserves useful identifiers, documentation requirements and configuration links. A design that loses material identity during production or repair makes future due diligence, recycling and substitution harder.

Trade-off management

Resilience is not the same as maximum redundancy

More suppliers, more inventory or more alternative materials can increase complexity, cost and qualification workload. Eco-design therefore requires a balanced decision: reduce concentration and lock-in where it matters most, but do not create uncontrolled variation.

Design movePotential resilience benefitEngineering caution
Dual-source interfaceReduces sole-source dependenceRequires equivalent qualification and configuration control
Lower material intensityReduces exposure to constrained inputMust preserve fatigue, reliability and performance margins
Modular critical-material unitLocalises future changeAdds interfaces, mass or integration constraints
Recycled / secondary feedstockMay reduce primary extraction dependenceQuality, contaminants, provenance and process qualification matter
Alternative material familyCreates future sourcing optionMay affect corrosion, joining, thermal behaviour, mass or certification
EU resilience connection: the Critical Raw Materials Act aims to reduce supply-disruption risk, diversify imports, incentivise technological progress and resource efficiency, and improve circularity. Ethical Armor translates those system-level goals into engineering questions at component level.
06
Final design logic.

Replacing Supplier B with Supplier A may solve today's sourcing concern but leave the architecture dependent on the same constrained material and one specialised process. A stronger response can combine a safer near-term route with a redesign that reduces intensity, preserves a second qualified source and isolates the critical-material subassembly for future change.

Knowledge check

Is the goal to eliminate all critical materials? No. Some materials are critical precisely because they provide difficult-to-replace performance. The goal is to understand the dependency and design the most resilient, traceable and technically robust architecture feasible.

You did not just choose a material. You designed its supply chain.
