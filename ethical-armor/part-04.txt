<section class="screen" data-guide="Deep theory four. Human-rights due diligence is ongoing. Review impact assessment, integration, tracking, communication, remediation, fundamental labour rights and why community and Indigenous rights may require specific attention." id="t4">
<div class="head"><div><div class="stepno">Theory after Milestone 4 · Deep dive</div><h2>Human-rights due diligence is an ongoing management process</h2><p>The goal is not to award a permanent “ethical supplier” label. It is to understand actual and potential impacts, act on the most serious risks, track whether controls work, communicate appropriately and provide or cooperate in remediation where responsibility requires it.</p></div></div>
<div class="guide-strip"><img alt="ARIA" src="aria.svg"/><div><b>ARIA · Core idea</b><span>People can be affected long before a material reaches aerospace production. Good due diligence asks whose rights may be affected, how severe the impact could be, what evidence exists and what leverage the company can use.</span></div><button class="btn voice" data-speak="t4">▶ Hear ARIA</button></div>
<div class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">UN Guiding Principles logic</div><h3>From policy commitment to action</h3><p>The UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights establish the corporate responsibility to respect human rights and frame human-rights due diligence as a process for identifying and addressing actual and potential adverse impacts. In practice, that means building the process into normal business and engineering decisions rather than treating it as a one-off sustainability questionnaire.</p>
<div class="framework"><div class="fw"><b>1</b><span>Assess actual and potential human-rights impacts.</span></div><div class="fw"><b>2</b><span>Integrate findings into relevant functions and act.</span></div><div class="fw"><b>3</b><span>Track whether the response is effective.</span></div><div class="fw"><b>4</b><span>Communicate how impacts are being addressed.</span></div><div class="fw"><b>5</b><span>Enable or cooperate in remediation where appropriate.</span></div></div></div>
<div class="theory-grid">
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Severity</div><h3>Prioritise the most serious impacts</h3><p>Not every problem has the same human consequence. A credible process considers the scale of harm, how many people may be affected and whether the harm can be remedied. Severe risks should not be downgraded merely because the supplier is strategically important or the component is urgent.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Affected people</div><h3>Go beyond the direct workforce</h3><p>Potentially affected groups may include mine workers, contractor labour, nearby communities, artisanal miners, women and children, Indigenous Peoples, land users and people exposed to private or public security arrangements. A supply-chain assessment that only asks about Tier 1 employees can miss the most serious upstream impacts.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Labour rights</div><h3>Fundamental rights at work</h3><p>The ILO's fundamental principles and rights at work cover freedom of association and collective bargaining, elimination of forced labour, abolition of child labour, elimination of discrimination, and a safe and healthy working environment. These provide an important lens for evaluating extraction and processing risks.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Communities and land</div><h3>Land, resources and meaningful consultation</h3><p>Where Indigenous Peoples may be affected, international human-rights standards place particular importance on rights connected to land, resources, culture and participation. The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples includes free, prior and informed consent provisions in several contexts. For training, the key lesson is that a land dispute cannot be reduced to “the permit exists”; the quality of consultation, rights affected and unresolved grievances matter.</p></article>
</div>
<div class="theory-callout blue"><strong>Engineering relevance:</strong> if a design can only use a material route associated with unresolved severe risks, Engineering may have more leverage than it appears. Changing material intensity, opening an alternative qualified route or redesigning an interface can reduce dependency and make mitigation more credible.</div>
<table class="theory-table"><thead><tr><th>Evidence</th><th>Weak interpretation</th><th>Better due-diligence question</th></tr></thead><tbody>
<tr><td>“No child labour” checkbox</td><td>Supplier is clean</td><td>What sites, labour categories and subcontractors were assessed, by whom, and when?</td></tr>
<tr><td>Community consultation report</td><td>Community approved the project</td><td>Who participated, what rights/impacts were discussed, were dissenting views recorded, and were commitments tracked?</td></tr>
<tr><td>Grievance mechanism exists</td><td>Risk is controlled</td><td>Can affected people access it safely, are complaints investigated, and is remediation tracked?</td></tr>
<tr><td>Audit passed</td><td>No human-rights risk</td><td>What was the audit scope, methodology, sample, timing and limitation?</td></tr>
</tbody></table>
<div class="case-strip"><div class="case-num">04</div><div><strong>Example.</strong><p>If a credible allegation of forced labour appears upstream, the correct response is not simply “ask Procurement for a cheaper supplier”. The organisation needs to assess the evidence, severity and connection to the chain, then determine the appropriate action under its due-diligence process — potentially including urgent suspension or disengagement for severe risks, while preserving safe technical alternatives.</p></div></div>
<div class="knowledge-check"><b>Knowledge check</b><p>Does an audit certificate end human-rights due diligence? No. Due diligence is ongoing and must react to changing circumstances, new evidence and the effectiveness of mitigation.</p></div>
<div class="source-note">Learning basis: UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (OHCHR); ILO Declaration on Fundamental Principles and Rights at Work; UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples; OECD minerals guidance.</div>

<section class="screen" data-guide="Deep theory five. Conflict-financing risk depends on who controls the chain and who benefits from payments. Review armed-group support, security-force risks, false origin, bribery, money laundering and revenue transparency." id="t5">
<div class="head"><div><div class="stepno">Theory after Milestone 5 · Deep dive</div><h2>Mineral value flows can create conflict-financing risk</h2><p>The concern is not that every payment near an extraction site is suspicious. The concern is whether mineral extraction, transport or trade provides direct or indirect support to actors involved in illegal control, extortion, serious abuses or conflict.</p></div></div>
<div class="guide-strip"><img alt="ARIA" src="aria.svg"/><div><b>ARIA · Core idea</b><span>Follow the beneficiary, purpose and legal basis of the payment. The word “security” is not evidence by itself; neither is a payment automatically illicit because security forces are involved.</span></div><button class="btn voice" data-speak="t5">▶ Hear ARIA</button></div>
<div class="theory-lead">
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">1 · Follow the value</div><h3>Who receives the economic benefit?</h3><p>Trace payments, fees, taxes, royalties, transport charges, security contracts and trader margins. Identify the beneficiary, ownership, contract, legal basis and reason for payment. Where an intermediary is used, determine what service it actually provides and who ultimately controls it.</p><p>The OECD framework distinguishes legitimate, legally required taxes, fees and royalties from support to non-state armed groups or security forces involved in illegal control, taxation or extortion. Therefore, the analysis must be evidence-based.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">2 · Follow the control</div><h3>Who controls mines, routes and trade points?</h3><p>Conflict-financing risk can arise where armed groups or abusive security forces control access to mine sites, transport routes, trading points or upstream companies; illegally tax or extort money or minerals; or receive logistical assistance, equipment or other support from the mineral trade.</p><p>A technically compliant material can therefore still carry a serious sourcing risk if the economic chain contributes to these activities.</p></article>
</div>
<div class="theory-grid three">
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Signal A</div><h3>Unexplained intermediary</h3><p>A payment passes through an entity with unclear ownership, no clear service and no transparent contractual purpose. This requires investigation of the beneficiary and transaction rationale.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Signal B</div><h3>Security payment</h3><p>The term itself is not enough. Ask whether the security force is lawfully engaged, screened, accountable, proportionate and linked to any illegal control, extortion or abuse.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Signal C</div><h3>False origin / payments</h3><p>Bribery, fraudulent origin records, misrepresented royalties or laundering of mineral revenues can undermine the entire traceability system and conceal higher-risk links.</p></article>
</div>
<div class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">OECD response logic</div><h3>Some risks require stronger responses than ordinary supplier improvement</h3><p>The OECD model policy recommends immediate suspension or discontinuation of engagement where there is a reasonable risk of sourcing from or linkage to parties committing serious abuses, or of direct/indirect support to non-state armed groups. For certain security-force, bribery, money-laundering and payment-transparency risks, the framework provides for measurable mitigation plans and escalation where improvement fails.</p><div class="theory-callout red"><strong>Training point:</strong> the exact response depends on the risk category, evidence, the company's position in the chain and applicable internal/legal procedures. Ethical Armor teaches the logic of escalation; it is not a substitute for a real legal determination.</div></div>
<div class="case-strip"><div class="case-num">05</div><div><strong>Milestone logic.</strong><p>The concerning transaction was not selected because “security” sounds negative. It was selected because the payment route, beneficiary and purpose could not be reconciled with the declared security arrangement, creating a serious escalation signal that required further investigation before sourcing continued.</p></div></div>
<div class="knowledge-check"><b>Knowledge check</b><p>Is every government tax or security payment a conflict-financing risk? No. The due-diligence question is whether payments are lawful, transparent and proportionate, and whether they support or are connected to illegal control, extortion, serious abuse or prohibited armed-group support.</p></div>
<div class="source-note">Learning basis: OECD Annex II model supply-chain policy, including serious abuses, armed-group support, security-force risks, bribery/false origin, money laundering and taxes/fees/royalties.</div>
