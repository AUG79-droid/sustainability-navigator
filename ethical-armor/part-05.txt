<section class="screen" data-guide="Deep theory six. Eco-design can reduce supply lock-in through material efficiency, substitution readiness, modularity, multi-source compatibility, circularity and design-for-information while preserving aerospace qualification." id="t6">
<div class="head"><div><div class="stepno">Theory after Milestone 6 · Deep dive</div><h2>Eco-design can redesign the sourcing problem</h2><p>Ethical sourcing becomes stronger when Engineering reduces structural dependency instead of leaving Procurement to manage a design that has only one technically possible supply route.</p></div></div>
<div class="guide-strip"><img alt="ARIA" src="aria.svg"/><div><b>ARIA · Core idea</b><span>The most resilient supply chain is often the one the architecture does not over-constrain. Engineering can preserve future sourcing options without relaxing safety, performance or qualification.</span></div><button class="btn voice" data-speak="t6">▶ Hear ARIA</button></div>
<div class="theory-grid three">
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Lever 1</div><h3>Reduce material intensity</h3><p>Revisit geometry, topology, magnetic architecture, thickness, yield loss and manufacturing route to determine whether the same function can be achieved with less of a constrained material. The objective is not “use less at any cost”; it is to remove unnecessary dependence while preserving technical margins.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Lever 2</div><h3>Design for substitution</h3><p>Where feasible, specify the performance envelope and interfaces so future alternatives can be evaluated without redesigning the whole system. This can include alternative grades, processing routes, recycled feedstock or material families — but only after the required qualification and safety evidence.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Lever 3</div><h3>Modularity</h3><p>Concentrate material-intensive functions in replaceable modules so future substitutions, obsolescence fixes or alternative sourcing routes remain local rather than propagating through the complete platform architecture.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Lever 4</div><h3>Multi-source compatibility</h3><p>A technically open interface can make it possible to qualify more than one credible route. This improves resilience and bargaining leverage, but it requires configuration discipline so alternatives remain equivalent and controlled.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Lever 5</div><h3>Circularity and secondary supply</h3><p>The Critical Raw Materials Act explicitly links secure supply with resource efficiency and circularity. Depending on the material and aerospace requirements, recycled content, recovery, reuse or closed-loop strategies may reduce primary-material exposure — but aerospace quality, contamination, provenance and certification constraints still apply.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Lever 6</div><h3>Design for information</h3><p>Material traceability is easier when the product definition preserves useful identifiers, documentation requirements and configuration links. A design that loses material identity during production or repair makes future due diligence, recycling and substitution harder.</p></article>
</div>
<div class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Trade-off management</div><h3>Resilience is not the same as maximum redundancy</h3><p>More suppliers, more inventory or more alternative materials can increase complexity, cost and qualification workload. Eco-design therefore requires a balanced decision: reduce concentration and lock-in where it matters most, but do not create uncontrolled variation.</p>
<table class="theory-table"><thead><tr><th>Design move</th><th>Potential resilience benefit</th><th>Engineering caution</th></tr></thead><tbody>
<tr><td>Dual-source interface</td><td>Reduces sole-source dependence</td><td>Requires equivalent qualification and configuration control</td></tr>
<tr><td>Lower material intensity</td><td>Reduces exposure to constrained input</td><td>Must preserve fatigue, reliability and performance margins</td></tr>
<tr><td>Modular critical-material unit</td><td>Localises future change</td><td>Adds interfaces, mass or integration constraints</td></tr>
<tr><td>Recycled / secondary feedstock</td><td>May reduce primary extraction dependence</td><td>Quality, contaminants, provenance and process qualification matter</td></tr>
<tr><td>Alternative material family</td><td>Creates future sourcing option</td><td>May affect corrosion, joining, thermal behaviour, mass or certification</td></tr>
</tbody></table></div>
<div class="theory-callout blue"><strong>EU resilience connection:</strong> the Critical Raw Materials Act aims to reduce supply-disruption risk, diversify imports, incentivise technological progress and resource efficiency, and improve circularity. Ethical Armor translates those system-level goals into engineering questions at component level.</div>
<div class="case-strip"><div class="case-num">06</div><div><strong>Final design logic.</strong><p>Replacing Supplier B with Supplier A may solve today's sourcing concern but leave the architecture dependent on the same constrained material and one specialised process. A stronger response can combine a safer near-term route with a redesign that reduces intensity, preserves a second qualified source and isolates the critical-material subassembly for future change.</p></div></div>
<div class="knowledge-check"><b>Knowledge check</b><p>Is the goal to eliminate all critical materials? No. Some materials are critical precisely because they provide difficult-to-replace performance. The goal is to understand the dependency and design the most resilient, traceable and technically robust architecture feasible.</p></div>
<div class="bigquote">You did not just choose a material. You designed its supply chain.</div>
