<section class="screen" data-guide="Deep theory one. Engineering material choices can create long-term supply-chain path dependency. Review how technical qualification, critical-material exposure, traceability and future sourcing optionality interact before design freeze." id="t1">
<div class="head"><div><div class="stepno">Theory after Milestone 1 · Deep dive</div><h2>Material selection creates supply-chain consequences</h2><p>Engineering does more than choose a material property. By freezing a specification, an interface and a qualification route, it can also narrow the future set of suppliers, processing routes and origins that Procurement is able to use.</p></div></div>
<div class="guide-strip"><img alt="ARIA" src="aria.svg"/><div><b>ARIA · Core idea</b><span>Responsible sourcing is not “buy the expensive option”. It combines technical qualification, evidence-based due diligence and design choices that preserve credible future sourcing options.</span></div><button class="btn voice" data-speak="t1">▶ Hear ARIA</button></div>
<div class="theory-lead">
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">1 · Why Engineering matters</div><h3>Design freeze can create path dependency</h3><p>A material decision can become difficult to reverse once geometry, loads, coatings, heat treatment, interfaces, manufacturing processes and qualification evidence are built around it. The longer the product remains in service, the more expensive a late material change may become.</p><p>That means a sourcing problem discovered years later may actually originate in an early engineering decision. If only one material grade, one proprietary process or one qualified source can satisfy the frozen design, Procurement has limited leverage when a disruption or social-risk issue appears.</p><h4>Questions before freeze</h4><ul><li>Does the requirement define the <em>performance needed</em>, or does it unnecessarily lock a specific material/process?</li><li>Is there more than one technically credible supply route?</li><li>Can origin and processing information be obtained with enough confidence for due diligence?</li><li>Would a future change require local requalification, or a platform-wide redesign?</li></ul></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">2 · Critical raw materials</div><h3>“Critical” describes supply exposure — not morality</h3><p>The EU Critical Raw Materials Act frames critical raw materials around economic importance and supply risk, and aims to improve security, resilience, resource efficiency and circularity across the value chain. A material may be “critical” because supply is concentrated, demand is growing quickly or alternatives are difficult — not because the material itself is unethical.</p><p>For an engineer, this changes the design question. The issue is no longer only “does this material meet strength, temperature or magnetic performance?” but also “what dependency does this choice create over decades of production and support?”</p><div class="theory-callout blue"><strong>Engineering translation:</strong> criticality is a design-resilience input. It should trigger questions about material intensity, substitution potential, recycled feedstock, repairability, stock strategy and multi-source compatibility — always within safety and qualification constraints.</div></article>
</div>
<div class="theory-grid three">
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Technical lens</div><h3>Can it perform?</h3><p>Strength, fatigue, corrosion, temperature, magnetic performance, manufacturability, reliability, certification and configuration control remain non-negotiable. Ethical Armor never asks the learner to bypass those requirements.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Supply lens</div><h3>Can it remain available?</h3><p>Look at concentration risk, sole-source exposure, long lead times, processing bottlenecks, geopolitical vulnerability and whether a second route could be qualified without major redesign.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Due-diligence lens</div><h3>Can we understand the chain?</h3><p>Traceability, supplier transparency, credible origin information, audit coverage and the ability to act on risks determine whether the organisation can perform meaningful due diligence rather than simply collect declarations.</p></article>
</div>
<div class="theory-callout warn"><strong>Regulatory boundary:</strong> Regulation (EU) 2017/821 establishes due-diligence obligations for covered Union importers of tin, tantalum, tungsten and gold (3TG). It should not be presented as a blanket legal rule for titanium or all rare-earth materials. This game uses the broader OECD minerals due-diligence method for the fictional titanium/rare-earth case.</div>
<div class="case-strip"><div class="case-num">01</div><div><strong>Engineering example.</strong><p>Two routes meet the same mechanical requirement. Route A is slightly slower but offers strong upstream evidence and a qualified alternative processor. Route B is faster and cheaper but origin evidence stops at the refiner. The right first action is not “choose A because it sounds ethical”; it is to understand the unresolved evidence, assess material criticality and preserve technically qualified options before freezing the architecture.</p></div></div>
<div class="knowledge-check"><b>Knowledge check</b><p>If a material is cheaper and technically superior, does that automatically make it the best engineering choice? No. Technical value must be considered together with lifecycle availability, traceability, qualification constraints and material-dependency risk.</p></div>
<div class="source-note">Learning basis: OECD minerals due-diligence guidance; Regulation (EU) 2017/821; Regulation (EU) 2024/1252. Formal applicability must always be validated for the real programme and legal context.</div>
