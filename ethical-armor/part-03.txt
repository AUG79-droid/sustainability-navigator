<section class="screen" data-guide="Deep theory two. Traceability means connected evidence across entities, custody and transformations. Review evidence types, common traceability gaps and the five questions that should be asked at each supply-chain node." id="t2">
<div class="head"><div><div class="stepno">Theory after Milestone 2 · Deep dive</div><h2>Traceability is a chain of evidence</h2><p>A robust answer is not “our Tier 1 supplier is European”. A component may pass through alloy producers, traders, processors, smelters/refiners and extraction sites before it reaches Tier 1. What matters is whether the evidence remains coherent across those transformations.</p></div></div>
<div class="guide-strip"><img alt="ARIA" src="aria.svg"/><div><b>ARIA · Core idea</b><span>Traceability is not a map with five boxes. It is the ability to connect material identity, custody, transformation and origin using evidence that is consistent enough to support a decision.</span></div><button class="btn voice" data-speak="t2">▶ Hear ARIA</button></div>
<div class="theory-lead">
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">1 · Chain of custody</div><h3>Who had the material, and what happened to it?</h3><p>Regulation (EU) 2017/821 defines a chain-of-custody or supply-chain traceability system as a record of the sequence of entities that possess minerals and metals as they move through the supply chain. In practice, an aerospace material route can also involve important transformations: ore becomes concentrate, metal, alloy, billet, forging, powder, magnet, semi-finished product and finally a component.</p><p>Each transformation creates a potential evidence break. The investigator therefore needs to connect the incoming material, the outgoing material and the responsible entity at each stage.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">2 · Evidence quality</div><h3>Not all documents answer the same question</h3><p>A supplier questionnaire may describe policy. A purchase order may identify a commercial relationship. A material certificate may identify grade and heat/lot data. A refiner record may support processing history. An independent audit may evaluate controls. None of these, by itself, proves every upstream fact.</p><p>The important question is: <strong>what claim does this document actually support?</strong> Good due diligence avoids using a document outside its evidentiary scope.</p></article>
</div>
<table class="theory-table"><thead><tr><th>Evidence type</th><th>What it may support</th><th>Typical limitation</th></tr></thead><tbody>
<tr><td>Supplier self-declaration</td><td>Policy, declared origin, declared upstream controls</td><td>May not be independently verified</td></tr>
<tr><td>Material / batch certificate</td><td>Material identity, grade, lot or heat continuity</td><td>May stop at the processor and not show extraction origin</td></tr>
<tr><td>Chain-of-custody record</td><td>Sequence of entities and custody/transformation</td><td>Can still contain gaps or unmatched identifiers</td></tr>
<tr><td>Independent audit / assurance</td><td>Whether specified controls were tested within a stated scope</td><td>Scope, date and sampled sites matter; “audited” is not universal proof</td></tr>
<tr><td>Extraction-site evidence</td><td>Mine/site identity and upstream origin evidence</td><td>Must still be matched to the actual batch or material route</td></tr>
</tbody></table>
<div class="theory-grid">
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">3 · Common gap</div><h3>Identity gap</h3><p>The paperwork exists, but identifiers do not reconcile: different batch numbers, dates, quantities or material descriptions. The chain may be real, but the evidence does not reliably connect this specific material.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">4 · Common gap</div><h3>Origin gap</h3><p>The refiner or trader is known, but the extraction source cannot be identified with confidence. This is exactly the contradiction used in Milestone 2.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">5 · Common gap</div><h3>Assurance gap</h3><p>The supplier states that due diligence is complete, but the audit is outdated, narrow in scope, limited to one facility or unable to test the highest-risk upstream stage.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">6 · Common gap</div><h3>Temporal gap</h3><p>A once-valid assessment may no longer represent the current route after a supplier, ownership, mine, processor, country context or security situation changes.</p></article>
</div>
<div class="theory-callout"><strong>Five questions for every node:</strong> Who is the entity? What did it receive? What did it transform or transfer? What evidence connects incoming and outgoing material? What due-diligence control covered the relevant risk and time period?</div>
<div class="case-strip"><div class="case-num">02</div><div><strong>Why Milestone 2 mattered.</strong><p>Tier 1 declared “upstream traceability complete”, yet the refiner evidence could not map the batch to a single extraction source. The contradiction is not proof of abuse. It is proof that the confidence level claimed by Tier 1 is not supported by the available evidence — therefore more investigation is required.</p></div></div>
<div class="knowledge-check"><b>Knowledge check</b><p>Can a chain be fully mapped and still have weak traceability? Yes. A diagram can be complete while the evidence behind one or more links is incomplete, inconsistent, outdated or too broad to support the claimed origin.</p></div>
<div class="source-note">Learning basis: OECD five-step framework, including management systems, traceability/controls, risk assessment, risk response, audit and reporting; Regulation (EU) 2017/821 definition of chain of custody/traceability.</div>

<section class="screen" data-guide="Deep theory three. Red flags trigger enhanced due diligence rather than automatic guilt. Review context, supplier and transaction signals, the OECD Annex II risk families and how findings should lead to proportionate risk responses." id="t3">
<div class="head"><div><div class="stepno">Theory after Milestone 3 · Deep dive</div><h2>Red flags are triggers for due diligence — not verdicts</h2><p>Good investigators separate context, indicators, verified findings and assumptions. That prevents two opposite errors: ignoring meaningful warning signs and treating every warning sign as proof of misconduct.</p></div></div>
<div class="guide-strip"><img alt="ARIA" src="aria.svg"/><div><b>ARIA · Core idea</b><span>A red flag changes the level of scrutiny. It tells you where normal evidence is no longer enough and where enhanced due diligence may be required.</span></div><button class="btn voice" data-speak="t3">▶ Hear ARIA</button></div>
<div class="theory-grid three">
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Context</div><h3>Where is the chain operating?</h3><p>Conflict-affected and high-risk areas can include armed conflict, fragile post-conflict settings, weak governance or security, and widespread or systematic violations of international law. Context elevates risk; it does not by itself prove that a specific supplier committed abuse.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Supplier / entity</div><h3>Who are we dealing with?</h3><p>Unknown beneficial ownership, unexplained intermediaries, repeated refusal to provide basic records, audit scope gaps or inconsistent declarations can justify stronger verification.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Transaction</div><h3>What does the money or material flow show?</h3><p>Unusual payment routes, unexplained cash-like fees, mismatched quantities, origin changes, undocumented traders or contradictory tax/royalty information may require escalation.</p></article>
</div>
<div class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">OECD Annex II risk families</div><h3>What serious risks does responsible-mineral due diligence look for?</h3><p>The OECD model supply-chain policy identifies several categories that companies should assess and address in conflict-affected and high-risk mineral chains.</p>
<table class="theory-table"><thead><tr><th>Risk family</th><th>Examples of what the framework looks for</th><th>Why it matters</th></tr></thead><tbody>
<tr><td>Serious abuses</td><td>Forced labour, worst forms of child labour, torture, widespread sexual violence, other gross abuses, war crimes or crimes against humanity</td><td>These are among the most severe adverse impacts in the framework</td></tr>
<tr><td>Support to non-state armed groups</td><td>Payments, equipment, logistics or sourcing relationships that directly or indirectly support armed groups controlling mines, routes or trade points</td><td>Mineral trade can contribute to conflict financing</td></tr>
<tr><td>Public / private security forces</td><td>Illegal control, taxation or extortion; inadequate accountability or screening</td><td>Security arrangements can create or intensify human-rights risk</td></tr>
<tr><td>Bribery / false origin</td><td>Bribes or fraudulent misrepresentation used to hide origin, taxes, fees or royalties</td><td>False information destroys the reliability of the due-diligence system</td></tr>
<tr><td>Money laundering / revenue transparency</td><td>Money-laundering risk or failure to account for taxes, fees and royalties</td><td>Financial opacity can conceal conflict-linked or illicit flows</td></tr>
</tbody></table></div>
<div class="theory-lead">
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Indicator → finding</div><h3>Build the evidence chain</h3><p>An indicator should generate a question: “what additional evidence would confirm or refute this?” A finding requires evidence with a defined scope, source and level of confidence. The organisation should document what is known, what remains uncertain and why the uncertainty matters.</p><p>This is especially important in engineering reviews: a decision should not be based on reputation, assumptions about a country, or a colour-coded dashboard with no supporting evidence.</p></article>
<article class="theory-block"><div class="theory-kicker">Finding → response</div><h3>Match the action to the severity</h3><p>Possible responses include requesting stronger evidence, increasing audit scope, creating a measurable mitigation plan, using leverage with the supplier, temporarily suspending trade, or disengaging when serious risk cannot be acceptably mitigated. The OECD framework explicitly recognises different response strategies depending on the risk and the company's position in the chain.</p></article>
</div>
<div class="theory-callout warn"><strong>Do not confuse risk geography with guilt.</strong> High-risk context is a reason for enhanced due diligence. It is not a licence to stereotype entire countries, communities or suppliers.</div>
<div class="case-strip"><div class="case-num">03</div><div><strong>Example.</strong><p>A supplier operates in a high-risk region but provides current site-level evidence, transparent ownership, independent assessment and a functioning grievance process. Another supplier operates in a lower-risk region but refuses to identify upstream traders and has conflicting origin records. The second case may create the stronger immediate due-diligence concern.</p></div></div>
<div class="knowledge-check"><b>Knowledge check</b><p>What is the purpose of a red flag? To trigger a deeper, proportionate investigation and risk response — not to act as proof of wrongdoing.</p></div>
<div class="source-note">Learning basis: OECD Due Diligence Guidance Annex I and Annex II; the EU Conflict Minerals Regulation uses conflict-affected/high-risk concepts for its covered 3TG scope.</div>
